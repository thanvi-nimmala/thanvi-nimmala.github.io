About Thanvi Nimmala

I’m a product designer based in the NYC metropolitan area. I like making design feel fun and useful, and I build the thing rather than a picture of it.

I graduated in 2026 with a B.A. in Information Technology & Informatics, and a minor in Digital Communication, Information & Media.

Since then I have been Product Design Lead on New Craft Society at CoCreate, where I took a capture tool from nothing to a public beta. I work end to end — research, flows, interface, design systems — and prototype in React and SwiftUI, so a decision can be tried instead of argued about.

I’m currently looking for full-time design work.

Outside of design I am usually painting, reading, or chasing a good playlist.

How I work
01
Ask before assuming
Talk to the people who will use it before drawing anything. The most useful thing you hear is usually the objection, not the compliment.
02
Turn what you heard into a bar
Findings become principles a design can be scored against, so the next decision is argued from the research rather than from taste.
03
Build it rather than draw it
Prototype in code. A version can be tried as software in days, and judged as the thing itself instead of as a picture of it.
04
Cut what has not earned its place
Plot the work against user value and build cost. Whatever costs the most and proves the least comes out, however far along it is.

The problem

Designers do archaeology on their own work.

By the time a case study is due, the process behind it is scattered across Figma, chat threads and a hundred tabs. Junior designers rebuild it from memory, on a deadline.
The product end to end, from its opening sequence through to a project open inside it.
Visit the live site ↗
The call

“I don’t think I’ll need it.”

Across 30+ interviews, the sharpest answer came from Hana, an interaction design junior at ArtCenter:
“I already do that in Medium. I write blogs and upload screenshots to document my process. I don’t think I’ll need it.”
Hana — junior in interaction design, ArtCenter.
She blogged because her portfolio felt too final. That was a workaround, not a preference. What designers were missing was a light work-in-progress layer — faster than writing a doc, and useful even if nobody else ever sees it.
Reading the interviews back, the same four demands came up whatever tool the person had already improvised with. I wrote them down as the bar every version would have to clear, so that the next decision could be argued from the research rather than from taste.
FOUR PRINCIPLES, FROM 30+ INTERVIEWS
INVISIBLE
Capture and documentation should be ambient and invisible.
EARN TRUST
Familiar instructions and mental models that build trust.
REFLECT
The tool should feel like insurance: when someone wants to revisit something, none of their process is lost.
VISUAL
Uniquely visual, with visual ways to interact with a capture and refine it for accuracy.
SHARED WITH ONE TEAMMATE

The turn

Save first, ask nothing.

The receipt shipped. ⌘⇧2 saves the capture before anything appears. The receipt says what happened and asks for nothing, and AI enrichment arrives later without blocking. It was the only version people kept using after the session ended.
The shipped capture tool, uncut — the hotkey, the receipt, and the summary arriving later.
SCOPE IT. SEQUENCE IT. CUT IT.
Research changed the roadmap too. Every feature had to earn its place through demonstrated user value and a realistic build cost, so I put the whole backlog on those two axes and we scored it together.
Auto capture had started as a P0. It came out of the exercise on its own, in the corner that costs the most to build and carries the least evidence that anyone wants it — because 30+ test sessions had raised four questions we couldn’t answer: what, when, who’s in control, and is it useful? It went back to exploration. Trust in onboarding took its place, from the opposite corner, which is where the research had been pointing the whole time.
OUTPUT
A scoped sequence, plus the confidence to remove work.
The system

Five ways in, one element, three views.

A capture tool is only as good as what it leaves behind. NCS takes five different kinds of input, and if each had arrived as its own kind of thing, every surface downstream would have had to learn all five.
FIVE WAYS IN
Deliberate when you want it, ambient when you don’t. Nothing leaves the Mac without your say.
PROACTIVE — You ask for it
ScreenshotsScreen recordings
AMBIENT — It is already there
Links you copyAI promptsText you highlight
ONE ELEMENT
All five become the same block. Same container, same anatomy, same behaviour, whether what landed in it is a sentence you highlighted or a recording of twenty minutes of work.
THREE VIEWS
Which is what makes the rest of the product cheap. A view is no longer a feature that has to handle five content types — it is an arrangement of one. Timeline orders the blocks by when. Moodboard arranges them by eye. Canvas puts them anywhere and lets you draw the relationships yourself.

The road back

The permission you cannot avoid asking for, and the worst moment to ask.

Trust and onboarding now sat at the top of the roadmap, and this product has a hard version of that problem. NCS cannot capture anything until someone grants screen recording, and it cannot sort a capture into the right project without access to Figma and the desktop. Ask for all three on the first screen and you have requested three favours before showing a single thing worth having.
The flow sketched end to end before anything was settled: sign in, permissions, hotkey, a test run, and the first saved capture.
FIVE VERSIONS, ONE QUESTION: WHEN DO YOU ASK?
I drew the happy path five times. The variable was never the screens, it was the order.
V1
Teach screen capture first
Explain what the tool does, then ask for what it needs to do it.
V2
Teach screen capture, ask in two parts
Same order, with the permission request broken up so it lands in smaller pieces.
V3
Capture first, sorting runs quietly behind it
Turn it around. Let someone capture something before anything is explained.
V3.5
Capture first, with a reason on every askshipped
The same shape, except each permission now says on screen what it buys you.
V4
Prove manual capture, prove automatic capture
Demonstrate both modes in a single run, to see whether one setup could carry both.
What survived was a sequence: challenge, then permission, then explain the product. By the time NCS asks for anything, it has already done something for you.
Read it against the four principles and the trade is legible. Earn trust pays for the reason on every ask. Invisible is why the sorting never surfaces as a step. Reflect is why the first capture has to land in a real project instead of a demo. And the skip branch is drawn because a flow that only works when everyone says yes is not a flow.
HANDED OFF AS ONE HTML FILE
I built the flow as a working prototype by directing an AI build. The prompts are the record of what I was actually deciding.
01
“Onboarding flow return”
Built out the full multi-stage sequence — arriving, welcome, grant, sorting, capture, test run, selecting, saved — with a stage picker to move between them while designing.
02
The polish pass
Cursor zoom and canvas drag on the welcome screen, a looping sort animation with real screenshot thumbnails, expo-out easing, arced flight paths, design-system shadows, and one title style held across all six screens.
03
“Remove the buttons that allow me to access the different parts of the flow”
The stage picker was mine, for building. Stripping it was the moment the prototype stopped being a tool for me and became a thing someone walks through in order.
04
“The thumbnail should match the screenshot the user takes”
A placeholder image would have quietly broken the one promise the flow is making.
05
“Provide it in html format so I can upload to my engineers”
It went over as a single HTML file they could open, rather than a prototype link and a spec to read beside it.

An exhibition that could only exist on the web.

Cyberfem Networks is an online exhibition of nine net-art works, from 1993 to now, that use the internet itself as their medium. I chose the works, wrote the curatorial text, and designed and hand-coded the site as a network you explore, in the visual language of the era the work came from.
Role
Curation, writing, interaction and sound design, and the build
Duration
2025–26
Context
Net Art Online, a course project
Platform
A static site, hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript
What I owned
All of it — the thesis, the selection, the writing, the interface.
Skills
CurationWritingArt directionInteraction designSound designHTML, CSS & JS
Overview

What happens when the network is the material, not the channel?

Most work about the internet is made somewhere else and put online afterwards. The nine pieces here cannot be moved: take away the jury’s submission system, the inbox, the frameset, the browser, and there is nothing left. That is the argument the exhibition makes, and the reason it had to be a website rather than a paper.
The exhibition as it opens: nine works wired to three threads, under a browser frame with a location bar and a scrolling ticker.
Visit the exhibition ↗
The works

Nine works, one medium, thirty years.

VNS MATRIX — ALL NEW GEN, 1993 TO 1994
A cyberfeminist game from the collective credited with helping coin the word, built around a non-binary protagonist sabotaging a patriarchal system. It is the period evidence that this argument is older than the platforms making it now.
MARTINE NEDDAM — MOUCHETTE, 1996 TO NOW
A website run by a thirteen-year-old girl who is not real, and has not aged in thirty years. It asks the visitor questions, invites replies, and keeps them. Thirty years of other people’s answers are part of the work now.
OLIA LIALINA — MY BOYFRIEND CAME BACK FROM THE WAR, 1996
A hypertext conversation between a couple after a war. Every click splits the browser into smaller frames, so the story fractures as you read it. The HTML frameset becomes the emotional form of the piece.
CORNELIA SOLLFRANK — FEMALE EXTENSION, 1997
Sollfrank invented hundreds of fictitious female artists and submitted their work to a museum’s first net-art competition. Two thirds of the entrants were women; all three prizes went to men. The piece reads the bias straight out of the institution that ran it.
OLD BOYS NETWORK — FIRST CYBERFEMINIST INTERNATIONAL, 1997
The first international cyberfeminist alliance, co-founded by Sollfrank. At documenta X it refused to define cyberfeminism and wrote a hundred anti-theses about what it is not instead.
SHU LEA CHEANG — BRANDON, 1998 TO 1999
The Guggenheim’s first web commission, built around the life and murder of Brandon Teena, a trans man in Nebraska. A multi-author space about gender, the body, surveillance and punishment, still running because the museum migrated its code.
SUBROSA — 1998 TO NOW
A collective working where bodies meet technology and labor: reproductive and genetic technologies, biotech, the information economy. Cyberfeminism as an organised practice, not a style.
LEGACY RUSSELL — GLITCH FEMINISM, 2012 TO 2020
An essay that became a manifesto. The glitch as refusal: a body that will not compute inside the gender binary, and the internet as a place to rehearse and build identity rather than only display it.
MOREHSHIN ALLAHYARI — SHE WHO SEES THE UNKNOWN, 2016 TO 2021
3D-modelled and printed re-figurings of female and queer jinn from Middle Eastern myth, alongside her idea of “digital colonialism” — who gets to scan, own and redistribute a culture’s heritage.
Opening a work opens a draggable window: a screenshot, the wall text, its threads, and a link out to the original.
Curation

The selection is the argument.

Nine works, chosen so that no two make the point the same way: a game, a persona, a hypertext, an intervention in a jury, an alliance, a museum commission, a collective, a manifesto, a set of 3D-printed figures. What they share is dependence — each needs a live network to be itself.
Instead of a chronology, I organised them around three threads that run through the whole field: Identity, Resistance, and Networked Bodies. Most works sit on two, so the connections between them carry as much of the argument as the works do. Sollfrank’s intervention and the alliance she co-founded are wired to each other directly.
Following the Identity thread: its works light up, everything off it dims.
Ending on Russell and Allahyari is deliberate. It shows the argument is not a 1990s period piece: the same questions moved into social media and 3D-scanned heritage, and they are still open. Two works deal with violence and self-harm, so they carry a content note before you leave for them, and every work links out to the original, still running at its own address, because sending people to the real thing is the point.
The build

The interface had to belong to the work, not to 2025.

A clean contemporary gallery layout would have flattened these pieces into content. So the site is written in the register of the work it shows: a browser frame with a location bar and a status bar, pixel and monospace type, magenta, green and cyan at full saturation on a near-black dotted grid. The curatorial statement is a README.TXT on the desktop.
The structure is the idea. Works are nodes, threads are hubs, and the wires between them are drawn live, so the exhibition’s argument is something you navigate rather than read about. Hovering a work traces its wires and the status bar reports that it is “connecting” to the work’s real address.
It sounds like the period, too. You enter through a dial-up gateway: pressing ENTER plays a modem handshake while a terminal prints the connection log, then the page dissolves into the network. Inside, every work has its own note, so running a cursor across the nodes or down the index plays a scale. Selecting a thread plays an arpeggio of the works on it, and windows open and close with system blips. All of it is synthesized live with the Web Audio API, with no audio files to load.
The way in. Browsers will not play sound until a visitor acts, so the ENTER click is both the threshold of the exhibition and the gesture that turns the sound on.
Sound is on by default but never forced: “enter without sound” sits under the button, the choice is remembered, and the gateway only appears once per visit.
An experimental interface cannot be the only way in. A second view lists the same nine works as an early-web directory listing, in date order and readable at a glance, and the threads filter it the same way they filter the network.
The Index view: the exhibition as a directory listing.
On a phone the network folds into a grid and windows become full-screen sheets. It is hand-written HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no framework and no build step, and adding a work is one entry in a data file. The exhibition should outlive its toolchain, the way the works it shows have outlived theirs.
On a phone: the works as a grid, and a work open as a sheet.
Reflection

What I would do differently.

1
Give the network a first path.
A network has no front door. A suggested route — start with one thread, end on the contemporary work — would help first-time visitors who do not know where to click.
2
Screenshots are not the work.
Each window shows a still of something that moves and answers back. Short recordings of each piece in use — answering Mouchette, clicking through Lialina’s frames — would show what the caption can only describe.
3
Let each work pull the page toward its own period.
The early-web treatment is right in principle, but it holds one house style across thirty years. The strongest version would let a 1993 game and a 2020 manifesto each change the room around them.

Five decisions that put a principal one minute from a program.

ArtsConnection has put teaching artists in New York City public schools since 1979, but its website was split across three domains, with broken links and no clear way to find a program. This is a concept redesign built around the people who book residencies. It is a current project, so the pages here are still being refined.
Role
Research, information architecture, wireframes, visual and accessibility design
Duration
Fall 2026, in progress
Context
Northeastern University web design course. A concept, not commissioned by or affiliated with ArtsConnection.
Platform
Responsive website, desktop and mobile
What I owned
All of it — the audit, the sitemap, the wireframes, the design system and every page.
Skills
Site auditInformation architectureWireframingVisual designAccessibilityFigma
Overview

A school leader should be able to find a program in under a minute.

The people who decide whether ArtsConnection comes to a school are principals and assistant principals, usually reading between meetings. I did the audit, the sitemap, the wireframes, the design system and every page. Five decisions shaped it.
The home page. One promise, one red button, and a finder that starts the search right in the hero.
Decision 01

Fold three websites into one.

Context. A main site, a teaching site and a teen site. Nav links for Teen Programs and Professional Learning pointed back to the home page, two sections were password-protected, and Performances opened a page from 2014.
Options. Fix each site where it is, or merge them under one navigation.
Result. One site in seven sections, with For Schools first, built from eight templates so the system stays small enough to maintain.
Before: one organization, three websites, each with its own header and navigation. Captured from the live sites as audited, fall 2026.
Below the hero, the page sorts visitors by audience, then by art form, then explains how a residency works — the order a school leader asks the questions in.
Decision 02

One promise, one button.

Context. Peer critique at the wireframe stage.
Options. Two hero buttons, or only the red request button.
Gave up. The second hero button.
Result. The headline became the plain promise “Teaching artists in every NYC classroom,” with one action and nothing competing with it.
Decision 03

Type that reads as New York, and reads for everyone.

Context. My first pairing looked like every generated landing page.
Options. Six pairings side by side, then eight headline faces against a fixed body face.
Result. Big Shoulders Display for headlines, a condensed face drawn for city signage. Atkinson Hyperlegible for everything else, designed by the Braille Institute for low-vision readers.
The style guide: type scale, palette, buttons and form states.
Decision 04

Change the brand red.

Context. White text on the original red measured 4.16 to 1, under the WCAG AA minimum of 4.5.
Options. Keep the red and avoid white text on it, or darken it.
Gave up. The original red, and yellow and sky blue as text colors. They are now only fills, tags and bars.
Result. White on the new red reaches 5.26 to 1, and the style guide lists twelve approved pairs. Art-form colors always come with a text label, and every mobile tap target is at least 44 points tall.
Before: the original red, with white text failing at 4.16 to 1.
After: the darkened red passes with white text at 5.26 to 1.
Decision 05

Show all eighteen programs, with no “Load more.”

Context. A peer asked in critique whether a “Load more” button would hide programs.
Options. Paginate the results, or show everything grouped by art form.
Gave up. A shorter page. Pills jump between art-form groups, and the filter sidebar stays in view.
Result. Filters apply as soon as you pick one, with no Apply button, and each card carries only what a principal compares.
Each card carries only what a principal compares: art form, grades, number of sessions and whether it runs in the school day or after school.

Showing people why a payment was stopped, instead of asking them to trust that it was.

When a bank holds a payment you learn two things: that it was held, and that you should call someone. What tripped it stays inside the bank. Keystone is a payments app built around a fraud model that explains its own decisions. It started as a Human-Computer Interaction final with a team, and I rebuilt it on my own two years later as a working front-end shell, which is where most of what follows actually got tested.
Role
UI/UX design and research on the original team; the rebuild on my own
Duration
2023, six weeks
Context
Human-Computer Interaction final project, Rutgers
Platform
Mobile, from a concept scoped as mobile and web. Built as a real React app rather than a clickthrough.
What I owned
The research and the interface, with a team, on the original coursework. The rebuild is mine alone: identity, motion, and the front-end shell.
Skills
Product designAI in the productInteraction designMotionFront-end buildDesign systems
Overview

Transparency, trust and control, in a flow where the model moves faster than the user.

The original concept had the right pieces: a security score, live alerts, biometric approval, and a model reading every transaction. It spent all of them on reassurance. Badges telling you that you were safe, which is the same black box in better packaging.
The questions I cared about were hard to answer in static frames. How much security presence is too much? Will anyone read five risk factors, or do they just want the verdict? So I rebuilt the thing as something you could use.
A payment sent, screened and approved. The prototype drives itself.
Open the working prototype ↗
The calm path

Designing for the ninety-nine payments that are fine.

Almost every time, the model has nothing to say. Screening should cost the user a beat of reassurance and no more, so the send flow runs five steps on one thumb with no interruption. Four checks complete before the money moves and you never wait on them.
Screening is the one moment the product acts on a payment instead of carrying it, so it is the one screen that changes register. The interface drains to near-black and a ticked dial turns while the payment is read. Every other screen in the app has colour in it. This one has almost none.
The instrument, turning. Three of four checks set.
Reading the risk

Leading with the stop, then the evidence.

A held payment usually arrives as a wall of text. But nobody opens that screen wanting to know why. They want to know how bad it is, and what they are supposed to do about it. So the review opens on the stop itself: the amount, the merchant, and the fact that nothing has left the account yet. The evidence follows, five weighted signals in order of strength.
Unknown merchant 94, unusual amount 86, distant origin 62, off-pattern timing 34, device match 12.
THE DECISION
A hold narrows the choice without taking it. Both outcomes sit in a footer pinned to the bottom of the sheet, so the decision stays within thumb reach however long the signal list runs. “Yes, this was me” releases the payment and folds the merchant into what the model trusts. “Not me” freezes the card and states plainly that you carry no liability while it is reviewed.
Research

Twenty people, five questions, asked out loud on campus.

The original research was deliberately low-tech. We stopped twenty people across the Rutgers campuses, asked each of them the same five questions about paying online, and typed the answers into a shared sheet. We asked out loud instead of sending a form, because people qualify themselves when they speak in a way they will not when they type, and the qualifications turned out to be the useful part.
We grouped the answers on an affinity diagram under needs, wants and concerns. The concerns column set the brief. What people described was less a fear of fraud than a fear of being told nothing while it was sorted out.
THE TENSION IT LEFT US WITH
Security you cannot see does no reassuring. Security you always see becomes a checkpoint. I did not want to settle that by argument, so in the rebuild I made visibility a variable with three settings and had every screen answer to it.

Identity

The first build looked generated, so I threw the surface away.

Version one was obsidian with an iris-to-magenta gradient, glows on everything, and a radar chart in the middle of the risk review. Every one of those is a default, the thing you reach for before you have decided what the product is. It read as competent and anonymous, which for a security product is the worst tone available.
A keystone is the wedge at the apex of an arch that locks the other stones in place. Pull it and the structure comes down, which is close enough to how layered protections work that the identity could be literal. Six stones, one for each protection you have armed, with the keystone at the top. Five of six set means the keystone is still out, so nothing is locked. The score is not illustrated by the stack. It is the stack.
The mark assembles the way the object does, bottom stone first, then it holds.
THE SAME SYSTEM, ACROSS THE FLOW
Six screens in the order you meet them: the promise, the dashboard, the ledger, the screening, the held payment, the score. One ground, one rock, one typeface, and an instrument that only shows up where the product is doing something to a payment.
The score you can move

Making the number answer to something the user controls.

Trust scores in finance apps are handed down, with no visible relationship to anything you can change. Here the score is the sum of six protection weights. Switch one off and it recalculates in front of you, the stack loses a stone, and the crown stone starts to drift because the structure is no longer steady.
96 down to 64 as protections come off, then back up.
ONE SCHEME, NOT ONE ACCENT
The palette is a single object. A scheme owns the ground, the hero gradient, the screening instrument, the rock the stones are cut from, and every surface and semantic colour when it runs dark. Swapping only the accent, which is what the first version let you do, produces the same app with an odd button in it.
Engineering

Two problems I only found because the prototype was real enough to use.

The first build let the decision buttons sit at the end of the content. On a 390-point screen the signal list pushed them below the fold, which put the most important control in the product behind a scroll past every reason to distrust it. A sticky footer and a gradient fade fixed it. Both outcomes now sit pinned to the bottom of the sheet. In a static frame that screen looked finished.
The held payment, with the decision pinned under the signals.
A CHART THAT MEASURED NOTHING
The dashboard carried a line chart of payments screened per week. It looked like insight. What it actually plotted was how much the account had spent, which is the user’s own behaviour and carries no decision for them to make, and it repeated the strip forty pixels below it.
It is one mark per payment now, across four weeks, laid out by day so that busy days crowd and quiet ones leave gaps, with height standing in for the amount. You read it as texture. The only two marks carrying colour are the payment that was held and the one still being watched.
The prototype

Eleven screens you can actually walk through.

Three onboarding beats, four tabs and four overlay flows. It opens on the welcome screen and you use it the way you would use the app, with no rail, no control panel and nothing else around the device. I built it in React so the questions this project asks could be answered by using it.
EVERY COLOUR AND CORNER IS ONE TOKEN AWAY
No component in the build holds a literal colour or radius. A scheme writes all of them as CSS custom properties that the whole app reads, so changing one line re-tints the product end to end. That is how the palette got from plaster and sage to slate without a single screen being redrawn.

Validation

What the build showed, and what it only asserts.

The finding that stuck: people will accept almost any safeguard once you tell them why it is there, and resent a small one when you do not. Every decision in the build follows from that. The stack you can move, the instrument that shows its working, the decision that stays on screen.

Reflection

What it has not earned yet.

1
The stack is a claim about legibility
I believe six stones read faster than a number, and that losing one reads as losing cover. That is a hypothesis. It needs five people and a stopwatch, not my confidence in it.
2
The dark scheme needs an accessibility pass
State is carried by colour, icon and text together, and I checked contrast by eye. It has not been through a screen reader, or tested past honouring the reduced-motion query.
3
The watching verdict needs a home
Recurring subscriptions sit between cleared and held, and that middle verdict currently gets a badge and one amber mark. It deserves a surface of its own.
4
Business mode is a switch, not a flow
Dual approval over a threshold is stated in the profile and implemented nowhere. It is the obvious next piece of work.
